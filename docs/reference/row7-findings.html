Row 7 — what it costs, what it buys, and where the real damage is

Row 7 refuses any entry within 15 trading sessions of an earnings print. The question was whether that is too strict. The answer is no — and the same evidence points somewhere the question didn't: at the positions you already hold.

read-only · candidate tier 18 board-days 2026-07-19 → 2026-08-10 · 848,328-row backtest frame · two adversarial passes, both of which cut against the first draft

1 · The deciding number

Row 7 exists to prevent one specific event: a single-session move so large that a close-basis stop cannot protect you. So the test is how often that event happens to trades that span a print, against trades that don't.

2.4× more likely — and it is not an artifact of longer holds. Spanning trades do run longer (34.5 sessions against 11.7), but the effect survives stratification: 24.1% vs 9.1% at 11–20 sessions, 25.8% vs 9.3% at 21–40, 26.7% vs 14.3% beyond 41. Within-stratum ratio ≈ 2.7×.
The mechanism, measured directly on 13,228 print sessions: volatility roughly doubles (sd 4.90% against a ~2.4% baseline), a 5%-or-worse fall is about 4.6× more likely (9.69% vs ~2%), and the deepest low of a 20-session hold lands on the print session 12.04% of the time against 5% by chance.

RMD is the case that justifies the row. A close-basis stop needs a close to act on and an open to fill at. An earnings print puts the entire move between those two prices. This is not a stop that failed; it is a stop that cannot apply.

3 · What the row costs

Row 7 appears in the failing set of 1,703 of 3,296 candidate observations — but in 1,529 of those another row was blocking anyway. Only the remainder is a real cost.

Those 69 arrive in earnings-season clusters, each name walking its runway down day by day: REITs and industrials 19–24 July (CTVA, FRT, KIM, O, ODFL, REG, SPG, UPS), energy 29 July – 4 August (DVN, FANG, OXY, APA, EOG, COP), tech and health 3–10 August (ANET, MSI, VTRS, CSCO, KEYS, LITE, ORKA).

Blast radius on the board today is zero. All three fail-opens are names that recently reported, so their true runways are 62–75 sessions. Fixing the rule would change no grade currently showing. ARWR's A+ is real: stored date 2026-08-06, true next print 2026-11-24.
But the board is not the whole map — and one off-board name is loaded. CRDO has a null cached date and a true print on 2026-09-02: 16 sessions away, crossing under the 15-bar in two. Its entry would read "unbounded runway" the whole way down. CYTK and HQ sit in the same state, one score-gate flip from being graded on a runway nobody checked.
The fix is not to fail closed. CRDO's true runway passes — blocking it would be a false negative of its own. The null is cache staleness, not vendor absence: the production fetcher returns the date on demand. The correct repair is to resolve on demand whenever the cached date is null or in the past, which costs nothing in participation and closes the hole. (The third case I had assumed — an unparseable date — turns out to be structurally unreachable: the only writer emits a strict date or nothing.)

5 · The finding the question didn't ask for

Row 7 gates entries. It says nothing about positions already held.

The evidence indicts the holding side far more than the entry side. Row 7's entire cost is 174 observations concentrated in one week. The holding gap's cost is a doubled catastrophic-day rate applying to every position that runs through a print — which, with a median hold of 10 sessions and quarterly reporting, is a routine occurrence rather than an edge case.

6 · The four arms that would settle it

Loosening the entry and fixing the holding are the same mechanism — "exit before the print" — applied at opposite ends. Testing either alone cannot separate them.

hold through the printexit N sessions before it
row 7 = 15 A — incumbent. What runs today. C — fixes the holding side only. My prior says this wins.
row 7 = 5 B — the naive loosening. More entries, print risk unchanged. D — enter close, exit before. More entries, no print exposure — but every trade duration-capped at ≤5 sessions.
Arm D has a structural problem worth pre-registering. The names a 5-session threshold admits carry 0–5 sessions of runway, so their holds are capped at 0–5 sessions against a median trade length of 10. Since the system's entire expectancy lives in its top 5% of outcomes, a hard duration cap removes the right tail by construction — the same mechanism Build 6A already measured, where 58.9% of touched trades improved and the dollars still lost.

What this does not settle, and what was corrected along the way

Two adversarial passes cut against the first draft, in every case reducing the claim. An asserted 6.5× increase in print-holding risk was really ~2.5×. A claim that the print explained "essentially all" of the deeper drawdown was wrong — it explains about 23%; three-quarters of the extra pain happens on sessions other than the print. A "+42% uplift" figure mixed two different bases. And the absolute trade counts vary by construction — an independent rebuild of the same recipe produced 13,236 trades against 11,664, so the rates are stable and the counts should not be quoted as measurements.

The return side of spanning is genuinely positive. Trades that spanned a print returned more on average (+0.54pp) with an identical hit rate, and a near-threshold control reproduced that. So row 7 is not free — it trades away real return for tail protection. Whether that exchange is worth making is a preference, not a measurement.

The analysis is day-level, not trade-level, for section 1. No sizing, no costs, and heavy window overlap means the raw sample sizes far exceed the effective ones. The trade-level −2R figures in section 1 are separately reconstructed with a real stop simulation.

And the fail-open population cannot be measured here. Those rows are precisely the ones excluded, because whether they spanned a print is exactly what is unknown about them.
