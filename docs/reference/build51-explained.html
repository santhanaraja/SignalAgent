Which of the seven rules is actually helping?

The A+ grade is seven rules stacked together. A stock only earns A+ if it passes all seven. This study asked a simple question about each one: is this rule keeping you out of bad trades, or throwing away good ones?

1 · How you test a single rule

You can't just delete a rule and compare — delete enough rules and "A+" becomes "every stock," so of course the average changes. The trick is to look at the near-misses.

2 · The headline: the "don't chase" rule is the expensive one

Rule 2 says don't buy a stock that has already run more than 1.8×ATR above its 20-day average. Sensible-sounding. Here is what the stocks it rejected went on to do.

The stocks this rule threw away outperformed the stocks it let through — by nearly double. And the rejected group is almost as large as the accepted one (23,893 vs 29,777), so this isn't a fringe effect. It also holds in both halves of the data, and it isn't driven by a few lucky names — the gain is spread across 527 different stocks, with no single one contributing more than 3%.

Two rules were vindicated, and that matters as much as the two that weren't. You asked last week whether the RSI ceiling should move to 80. The answer from the data is no — the stocks rejected on RSI went on to do worse, so that rule is earning its keep. The problem runs through the extension rule, not the RSI one.

4 · What this actually means

The A+ grade was built to avoid chasing. Buy the calm pullback, not the stock that has already run. That instinct is what the extension rule encodes — and it is the single most expensive rule in the set.

It fits everything else this project has measured. The earlier study found A+ stocks returned less than C stocks. Then the distribution work found that A+'s entire profit comes from a handful of big winners — strip the top 5% of days and the A+ group actually loses money. This study now names the rule responsible for cutting those winners off.

But nothing changes yet, and that restraint is deliberate. This test measures signals in isolation — no stops, no position sizing, no exits. The earlier system-level test (Build 5B) found that once real stops were applied, no entry rule was measurably better than any other. So a rule can look expensive here and still make no difference to a live account.

Next step, in one line: take the extension rule and the YTD penalty into a full system test — with stops, sizing and exits — and see whether removing them actually makes the account perform better, or just makes the spreadsheet look better.
